# Proposition 132: Penalties for fentanyl crimes

**On every Denver ballot.** Citizen initiative. Statutory. Passes with a **majority**.

[Back to the summary](../ballot-summary.md)

## What the choice does

Fentanyl is a synthetic opioid. It is legal as a prescribed pain drug and illegal to possess or distribute without a prescription. The Blue Book says it is the leading cause of drug-overdose deaths in Colorado and the United States because small amounts can kill and because it is mixed into other drugs. The measure also covers related synthetic opioids. Weight includes the whole mixture, not just the fentanyl in it.

A yes vote:

- Makes possession of **any amount** a felony. Possession under 1 gram can be reduced to a misdemeanor if the person completes court-ordered treatment. Possession over 1 gram is no longer eligible for that reduction.
- Requires **8 to 32 years in prison for distribution of any amount**, including selling, transferring, or sharing. Current law reserves that mandatory range for distribution to a minor, distribution over 50 grams, or distribution of at least 4 grams that causes death.
- Removes the “Good Samaritan” reduction for distribution cases. It keeps a Good Samaritan reduction for some low-level possession.
- Bars judges from considering some mitigating facts, including a reasonable belief that the substance did not contain fentanyl.

Blue Book sentencing comparison, shortened from Table 1:

| Offense | Current law | Proposition 132 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution over 50 g | Mandatory 8–32 years | Mandatory 8–32 years |
| Distribution 4–50 g | Possible 4–8 years; higher if death or a minor is involved | Mandatory 8–32 years |
| Distribution under 4 g | Possible 2–4 years; Good Samaritan reduction available | Mandatory 8–32 years |
| Possession 1–4 g | Possible 0.5–1 year; reductions for deniability or treatment | Possible 2–4 years |
| Possession under 1 g | No prison; higher charges after repeat convictions | Possible 0.5–1 year, with a path down after mandatory treatment |

People who today cannot be sent to state prison include some possession under 1 gram, some 1-to-4-gram possession where the person shows they did not know fentanyl was present, and some distribution under 4 grams covered by the Good Samaritan rule.

## Official fiscal note

Criminal-fine revenue: about **$23,000** in year one and **$73,000** a year after that.

State spending from the General Fund: **$0.9 million** in year one (courts and public defenders), **$8.9 million** in year two as prison terms begin, and **$68.2 million** by year five. The state may need more prison space. The measure names no funding source. County district-attorney costs also rise. Effects on health care, education, and higher education depend on later budgets.

## Arguments in the Blue Book

**For.** Fentanyl’s potency justifies penalties above other drugs. Mandatory prison for distribution is aimed at traffickers, including people who move mixtures that contain fentanyl, and at a sense of justice for families. The argument also says heavier user penalties may deter use.

**Against.** The argument says longer sentences are unlikely to cut use, crime, or deaths, and may stop people from calling for help in an overdose or from entering treatment. It says trace distribution would be sentenced like high-volume dealing, and that prison costs would crowd out treatment.

## What can swing a vote

| If this is the priority | It points toward |
| --- | --- |
| Any act of distributing fentanyl, at any weight, should carry at least eight years | Yes |
| Weight, a death, or distribution to a minor should still set the mandatory floor, as they do now | No |
| People should not fear a distribution charge for staying at an overdose they helped cause by sharing | No. The Good Samaritan reduction for distribution goes away. |
| Possession under 1 gram should be a felony unless treatment is completed | Yes |
| Prison cost on the path to $68.2 million a year by year five, with no dedicated funding | No, if that cost is unacceptable; the “for” side treats the cost as the price of incapacitating sellers |
| Overdose deaths as the harm to minimize | The two arguments disagree on whether these penalties reduce deaths. The Blue Book does not provide a Colorado estimate of deaths averted. |
