# Amendment 87: Graduated income tax

**On every Denver ballot.** Citizen initiative. It amends both the constitution and the statutes. The Blue Book says it **passes with a majority**, not the 55 percent required for a typical constitutional amendment. A private forecast has described the threshold as 55 percent. Use the Blue Book.

[Back to the summary](../ballot-summary.md)

Read this with [Proposition 136](proposition-136-income-tax-cap.md). If both pass, they conflict.

## What the choice does

Colorado has taxed income at one rate since 1987. TABOR requires that single rate. The current rate is **4.4 percent**.

Amendment 87 would, starting in 2027:

- Remove the constitutional single-rate rule.
- Tax Colorado taxable income in six brackets. Each rate applies only to the slice of income inside that bracket.
- Require the additional revenue to be spent on K-12 education, health care, and early childhood care and education, on top of current spending, not instead of it.
- Exempt that additional revenue from the TABOR limit. The Blue Book says existing TABOR refunds do not change, because only the new revenue is exempt and the limit itself stays put.
- Leave the tax on the gain from sale of a principal residence at 4.4 percent.
- Require an audited annual report. The legislature chooses the specific programs.

The brackets are **not** adjusted for inflation. Over time, more income falls into higher brackets even if purchasing power does not rise.

## Revenue

Staff forecast of new income-tax revenue: **$958.4 million** in FY 2026–27 (a half year) and **$1.972 billion** in FY 2027–28, then more as incomes and population grow. The ballot’s **$2.7 billion** figure is the statutory maximum estimate for the first full year, not the base forecast. One-time Department of Revenue cost: **$98,000**.

The “for” argument says the cut covers **97 percent of taxpayers**. That percentage is in the argument section, not in the staff tables above.

## If Proposition 136 also passes

Proposition 136 would cap the rate at 4.4 percent. Amendment 87’s top rates are above that cap. The Blue Book says that when voter-approved measures conflict, the one with more votes generally wins, “however, if both measures are approved by voters, the exact outcome is unclear.”

## Arguments in the Blue Book

**For.** Most households and small businesses pay less, only higher taxable incomes pay more, and a dollar means more to someone earning $30,000 than to someone earning $1 million. The new money backfills K-12, health care, and child care when federal changes put programs at risk. The federal government and 27 states already use graduated rates.

**Against.** A multibillion-dollar increase on higher incomes will push employers and high earners out of Colorado, and the unindexed brackets will reach more people each year. The flat tax is described as fair because high earners already pay more dollars and lower incomes already receive credits. The spending categories are broad, and the argument says the state should prioritize what it already collects.

## Where candidates and the current governor stand

Axios Denver, October 22, 2026:

- Victor Marx opposes it. Axios quotes his statement that nearly doubling the top rate will not make Colorado more prosperous.
- Phil Weiser has not taken a position. Axios reports that he cites a pending lawsuit.
- Governor Jared Polis, who is term-limited, opposes it and has called it a “HUGE blank check to politicians.”

## What can swing a vote

| If this is the priority | It points toward |
| --- | --- |
| A smaller state income-tax bill and your Colorado taxable income is under about $510,000 | Yes. The staff maximum cut is $325 a year. |
| Your taxable income is over $500,000 and you do not want the 7.4 to 8.4 percent slices | No |
| New, TABOR-exempt money that must add to schools, health care, and early childhood programs, with the legislature picking the programs | Yes |
| Keeping the single rate, and the rule that any future rate structure needs a clear voter decision rather than a conflict with Proposition 136 | No |
| Brackets that rise with inflation | This measure does not do that. That fact points toward No if indexation is a requirement for your vote. |
| A rate cap at 4.4 percent | Vote No on 87, and see Proposition 136. Voting Yes on both leaves the winner uncertain. |
