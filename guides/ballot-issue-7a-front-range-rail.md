# Ballot Issue 7A: Front Range passenger rail sales tax

**On Denver ballots.** Denver is inside the Front Range Passenger Rail District. Referred by the district board. The election notice is printed in the 2026 Blue Book, and the Blue Book says the notice is the district’s, not a Legislative Council analysis.

[Back to the summary](../ballot-summary.md)

## What a yes vote does

A yes vote imposes a **0.333 percent** sales and use tax, one-third of one cent on a one-dollar purchase, inside the district. The ballot states exemptions that already exist in Colorado law, including gasoline, food, residential electricity and gas, prescription drugs, and medical supplies.

The ballot’s TABOR questions, which are the district’s official figures:

| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Tax increase, first full fiscal year (the ballot’s maximum) | $295,000,000 |
| Partial year 2027, estimated in the fiscal table | $144,000,000 |
| Debt principal | $580,000,000 |
| Maximum annual debt repayment | $35,000,000 |
| Total stated repayment cost | $785,000,000 |
| District debt today | $0 |

The tax has **no sunset** in the ballot title. Revenue, contributions, and investment earnings would be a voter-approved revenue change. An independent auditor and a rotating group of resident taxpayers would review the dedicated revenue each year.

The purpose on the ballot is to construct, operate, and maintain Colorado Connector passenger rail linking communities including Pueblo, Colorado Springs, Sterling Ranch, Littleton, Denver, Westminster, Broomfield, Louisville, Boulder, Longmont, Loveland, and Fort Collins, and to fund station-area improvements and local connections.

## The five-year spending percentage

The TABOR table shows district fiscal-year spending rising from **$83,136** in 2022 to an estimated **$6,544,584** in 2026, a dollar change of $6,461,448 and a percentage change of **7,732 percent**. That percentage is the required calculation on a very small base. It is not the tax proposal. The tax proposal is the $295 million first-full-year figure, against estimated spending of $100,000 in that year without the tax.

## Comments printed in the notice

The Blue Book says it went to print before the deadline for every comment. Later comments, if any, are at [frprdistrict.com](https://www.frprdistrict.com). The summaries below are submitted comments. They are not Legislative Council findings.

**For, as summarized in the notice.** The comment describes I-25 congestion and a rail alternative for people who cannot or do not drive; stations near Denver sports venues; household vehicle costs “up to $10,000 annually”; an emissions cut “roughly equivalent to removing about 29,000 cars”; use of existing railroad corridors rather than all-new lines; starter service “on track to open in 2029”; service from Fort Collins to Pueblo “within five years”; and a **$1.5 billion** local-return program for station communities, including infrastructure, affordable housing, and other transportation projects. It says the tax is new money for rail and does not take highway funds.

**Against, as summarized in the notice.** The tax is permanent and is paid by people who never ride. The comment calls a sales tax regressive and says combined sales taxes in some communities are already 8 to 9 percent. It says voters are being asked to approve the tax before final costs, fares, schedules, ridership, railroad agreements, and full-corridor dates are known. It cites earlier regional rail taxes that ran for more than 20 years without service in some places, and communities that have paid more than $100 million toward prior plans without receiving service. It says starter service, described as three daily trips between Denver and Fort Collins, could go forward without this tax. It argues the train will not match door-to-door car trips, will not serve the airport directly, and could be no faster than driving or existing buses.

## What can swing a vote

| If this is the priority | It points toward |
| --- | --- |
| A dedicated Front Range rail tax, about one-third of a cent, with $580 million of borrowing authority | Yes |
| Waiting until fares, railroad agreements, and a demonstrated starter service exist | No. The “against” comment says starter service could proceed without the tax. That claim is not confirmed in the fiscal table. |
| The $1.5 billion local-return program and 2029 starter service | Yes, if you accept the “for” comment’s project description |
| A sunset, or a tax that only riders pay | The measure has neither. That points toward No if either is a requirement. |
| Past regional rail taxes that did not produce service | The “against” comment treats that history as the reason to vote No. The notice does not attach an audit of those earlier taxes. |
| Household sales-tax burden | Yes raises the rate by 0.333 points on taxable purchases. Food for home consumption and gasoline are among the stated exemptions. |

## Data gaps

This guide does not verify the 29,000-car figure, the $10,000 vehicle-cost figure, the $1.5 billion local return, the 2029 opening, or the claim that three daily Denver–Fort Collins trips can start without the tax. Those are comment claims. No Denver-only taxable-sales estimate of the household cost was calculated here.
