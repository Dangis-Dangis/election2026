# Amendment 81: Law enforcement notification to federal immigration authorities

**On every Denver ballot.** Citizen initiative. Constitutional. The Blue Book says it passes with **55 percent** of the vote.

[Back to the summary](../ballot-summary.md)

## What the choice does

The certified title asks whether the Colorado Constitution should require law enforcement to notify the U.S. Department of Homeland Security when a person is charged with a violent crime, or with any crime if the person has a prior felony conviction, if law enforcement cannot determine that the person is lawfully present.

The Blue Book staff analysis adds the operational detail:

- Notification is due within **72 hours of charges**, not at arrest, and before any finding of guilt.
- “Crime of violence” includes crimes causing serious bodily harm or death, or involving a deadly weapon. The analysis lists murder, assault, arson, escape, extortion, kidnapping, aggravated robbery, and certain sexual offenses and human trafficking.
- A **prior felony of any age** pulls in any new charge, including traffic violations, petty offenses, and misdemeanors.
- Officers, correctional officers, and district-attorney personnel must make a reasonable effort to determine lawful presence. The text does not define “reasonable effort” or “lawful presence.”
- Current Colorado law allows cooperation on federal criminal matters and limits participation in civil immigration enforcement, including sharing some nonpublic personal information. Denver has gone further and bars federal immigration authorities from some city-controlled property, including courthouses. El Paso County requires notification when an officer suspects a person in custody lacks legal status. Neither federal nor state law currently requires notification when charges are filed.

## Official fiscal note

Legislative Council Staff estimates **$50,000** for the Department of Revenue and **$10,000** for the Department of Natural Resources for system updates in FY 2026–27, assumed from the General Fund. Most of the new work falls on local district attorneys. The state does not publish a count of how many people this would cover.

## Arguments in the Blue Book

These are the nonpartisan booklet’s summaries of the two sides, not staff findings of fact.

**For.** People in the country unlawfully who are charged with a violent crime, or who have a prior felony and are charged with any crime, should be reported for possible federal enforcement, including removal. A single statewide rule would replace today’s patchwork.

**Against.** The duty attaches when someone is charged, not convicted, so it reaches people who are presumed innocent. Someone who is lawfully present, or who is later acquitted, could still be referred if status cannot be proved in 72 hours. Undefined terms leave room for decisions based on language or appearance, and that can discourage crime reporting.

## What can swing a vote

| If this is the priority | It points toward |
| --- | --- |
| A statewide duty to tell federal authorities about people charged with a violent crime, or any crime after a prior felony, when lawful presence is not confirmed | Yes |
| Waiting for a conviction, or for a judicial warrant, before immigration status is reported | No |
| Replacing Denver’s stricter local limits with one state rule | Yes |
| Keeping local governments able to limit civil immigration cooperation, as Denver does now | No |
| Concern that “any crime” plus an old felony includes low-level charges | No |
| Concern that violent charges and repeat felony records are not systematically referred today | Yes |
