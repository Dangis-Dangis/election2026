# Proposition NN: Keep revenue above the TABOR limit for education and children’s programs

**On every Denver ballot.** Referred by the legislature. Statutory. Passes with a **majority**.

[Back to the summary](../ballot-summary.md)

Read this with [Proposition 137](proposition-137-conservation.md) and [Amendment 87](amendment-87-graduated-income-tax.md). They move different piles of money.

## What the choice does

Proposition NN does not create a new tax rate. It lets the state keep revenue it already collects above the TABOR limit, up to a new, higher limit.

The new limit equals the current TABOR limit plus the highest amount the state has spent on K-12 from certain sources in prior years. In the first year that ceiling is **$4.6 billion** above today’s limit. Staff expects the state to collect only about **$500 million** above today’s limit, so the practical keep is about $500 million, not $4.6 billion. Revenue above the new ceiling would still be refunded. Staff does not expect collections to hit the new ceiling for at least five to ten years, absent a change in the forecast.

How the retained money is spent:

1. First, reimburse local governments for property-tax exemptions for seniors, veterans with a disability, and Gold Star spouses. That reimbursement is already a TABOR refund mechanism.
2. The rest goes to a new Children’s Account.
3. For ten years, money in the account must go to higher teacher pay and retention, career and technical courses, and smaller class sizes, distributed to districts and charter schools. If that is less than half the account, more must be spent on disability services, school services, and instructional hours so that at least half the account is K-12.
4. For ten years, what remains after the K-12 requirement goes to programs that support children, with priority on full-day preschool and child care.
5. After ten years, a flat K-12 amount continues for pay, career and technical education, and class size. **The rest may be spent on any purpose the legislature chooses.**

The ballot title’s phrase “increase two percent each year for the next ten years” is the growth rule inside that design. The Blue Book’s dollar path for the K-12 piece is about **$110 million** in year one and **$220 million** in year two, possibly **$1 billion to $2 billion** a year by year ten, and only if the retained revenue is there.

## What happens to refunds

Staff’s three scenarios:

- Revenue above the new limit: refunds shrink but do not disappear.
- Revenue above today’s limit and below the new one: refunds go to zero. Staff calls this the likely case for the next several years unless there is a recession.
- Revenue below today’s limit: no refunds would have been paid anyway, so NN changes nothing.

Estimated refund reduction: **$329.9 million** in FY 2026–27 and **$521.0 million** in FY 2027–28. Those two years’ refunds are eliminated under the forecast.

Spending shifts in the staff table include **$241.5 million** for K-12 and **$241.5 million** for children’s programs in the current year, and a **$153.1 million** reduction in money that would otherwise sit in the general budget, moved into the Children’s Account for FY 2026–27 and FY 2027–28.

## Interaction with Proposition 137

Proposition 137 removes sporting-goods sales-tax revenue from the TABOR limit. If both pass, less revenue is subject to the limit, so NN retains less, possibly nothing, depending on the year.

Amendment 87’s new income-tax revenue is separately exempt from TABOR. NN does not spend that money. NN spends revenue that is already in the current tax system and over the limit.

## Arguments in the Blue Book

**For.** A state study, cited in the argument, found a school funding shortfall of **$3.5 billion**, about **$4,000 per pupil**. The argument says NN uses money already collected, with an audit and district-level spending reports.

**Against.** Ending or cutting refunds is a tax increase in effect. The argument also says school funding rose 25 percent over five years while enrollment fell, and that the state should cut waste before keeping the refunds. A separate “against” point says that after the early years the state could control on the order of **$5 billion** a year. That figure is in the argument, beside the staff estimate that the new ceiling starts $4.6 billion higher while near-term collections above the old limit are about $500 million.

## Where the governor candidates stand

Axios Denver, October 22, 2026: Phil Weiser supports Proposition NN. Victor Marx opposes it, on the ground that it would erase TABOR refunds for the foreseeable future.

## What can swing a vote

| If this is the priority | It points toward |
| --- | --- |
| More state money for teacher pay, class size, career courses, preschool, and child care without a higher tax rate | Yes |
| Keeping the refund amounts in the table, which are small at lower incomes in the current two-year forecast and larger at higher incomes | No |
| A sunset, or a permanent ban on using leftovers for non-education programs | The measure does not have that sunset. After ten years the legislature may spend leftovers on any purpose. That points toward No if a sunset is required for your vote. |
| You are also voting Yes on Proposition 137 | The school dollars from NN get smaller. Decide whether the remaining NN retention is still enough. |
| Recession risk | In a year below the TABOR limit, NN does not take a refund, because there is no refund. It also does not create new cash. |
