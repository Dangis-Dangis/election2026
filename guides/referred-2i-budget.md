# Referred Question 2I: Budget calendar and an optional two-year budget

**On every Denver ballot.** Referred by City Council. Charter amendment. In the non-tax Ballot Information Booklet.

[Back to the summary](../ballot-summary.md)

## What a yes vote does

The charter’s default budget term stays **one fiscal year**. Council could, by ordinance filed by January 31 of the year before the term starts, choose a **two-year** budget term. That ordinance can be vetoed, overridden, or later repealed. A two-year term is optional.

If Council ever chooses two years, the charter text still requires an annual appropriation. In the planning year the mayor would submit a draft for year two. In the first year of the term the mayor would submit a final budget for year two, which Council could amend. The draft is “contingent upon annual appropriation.” Denverite’s voter guide states the same point: planning could run two years, while spending authority would still be voted every year.

Other changes apply even if Council never adopts a two-year term:

- The mayor’s proposed budget moves from September 15 to the **first Friday in September**.
- The proposed budget is due to Council on the **second Friday in October** instead of the third Monday in October.
- The charter’s July 1 preliminary estimate is removed. Agencies would still submit expenditure and revenue estimates, and the mayor would still prepare a budget calendar by May 1 and a six-year capital program.
- Section 7.1.8, on budget allotments, is marked repealed in the printed text.

The “for” comment, which goes beyond a single sentence of charter text, also describes a public input process run by the Mayor’s Office with results presented by the third Monday in July, and a new response when forecasts are off:

- If spending is expected to exceed appropriations by **2 percent** or more, or revenue is expected to fall short by **2 percent** or more, the mayor must propose reductions and tell Council.
- At **5 percent**, the mayor must propose reductions and Council must reduce appropriations by ordinance.

The against comment disputes that a two-year plan is useful. It does not dispute the date changes.

## Official fiscal note

One-time vendor work of about **$50,000**: $30,000 for the budget-development system (120 hours at $250) and $20,000 for the publishing system (100 hours at $200). No anticipated cost for moving the September date. The note says the city can still use a one-year budget “in time of economic uncertainty,” which matches the optional ordinance.

## Comments in the booklet

The clerk does not verify comments.

**For.** Longer planning for projects and vendors, staff time in the second year for evaluation, an earlier budget so Council and the public have about an extra week, a required community process before the mayor’s budget, and mandatory correction when the forecast is off by 2 or 5 percent.

**Against.** The comment says Denver faced a **$200 million** mid-year deficit “just last year,” that the general fund is **56 percent** sales tax, and that two crises since 2020 show why a 12-month forecast is already hard. It says the state requires a balanced budget every year, so a biennial plan is duplicated work. It says a two-year plan shifts power from Council to the mayor. It says fewer jurisdictions use biennial budgets, and more have left them than have adopted them. It says the city already has a six-year capital plan and three-to-five-year contracts, so the certainty argument fails.

The $200 million deficit and the 56 percent sales-tax share are inside the against comment. They are not in the Department of Finance fiscal note.

## What can swing a vote

| If this is the priority | It points toward |
| --- | --- |
| An earlier September budget and a charter trigger when the forecast is off by 2 or 5 percent | Yes. Those pieces do not depend on ever using a two-year term. |
| Never giving Council the option to plan in a two-year draft | No |
| Fear that a two-year ordinance, once passed, locks spending | The charter text still requires a final budget and an annual appropriation in year two, and Council can repeal the two-year ordinance. Denverite reports the same annual-appropriation point. |
| Forecasting error, using the against comment’s account of a $200 million gap | No, if you treat that history as decisive. The fiscal note does not confirm the $200 million figure. |
| The $50,000 system cost | Too small to swing either way unless you reject any new spending. |
