# Amendment 84: Identification number on mail ballots

**On every Denver ballot.** Citizen initiative. Constitutional. Passes with **55 percent**.

[Back to the summary](../ballot-summary.md)

## What the choice does

For federal and statewide elections, a voter would sign the mail-ballot envelope and add the last four digits of a Social Security number or of a Colorado driver’s license or ID number that complies with the federal Real ID law. This is **in addition to** today’s signature check.

If the number is missing or does not match the registration database, the county must notify the voter. The ballot is not counted unless the voter cures it within eight days after Election Day. Cure can be a copy of the required ID, or a U.S. passport, military ID, or tribal photo ID, in person or digitally. Voters with a qualifying disability, and elderly voters entitled to vote by mail under federal law, may use a broader set of documents.

In-person ID rules do not change. Those rules already allow more document types, including some that show name and address.

The Blue Book states that in **2024, 92 percent** of Colorado ballots were mail ballots, most of them returned to a drop box. Signature mismatches are reviewed by two election judges of different parties before a cure notice goes out.

## Official fiscal note

Up to **$2.7 million** in FY 2026–27 and **$1.2 million** a year after that, split between counties and the state. County costs up to $2.5 million in the first year and $1.2 million later; the state reimburses about 45 percent of county costs (up to about $1.1 million, then up to $500,000). The Department of State’s own outreach is estimated at $100,000 in FY 2026–27 and $200,000 in FY 2027–28, plus about $20,000 per statewide election later. Business filing fees may rise to cover the department’s share. The measure does not itself raise a tax.

## Arguments in the Blue Book

**For.** An ID number is more consistent than a signature comparison, most voters already have one of the numbers, and anyone who lacks it can vote in person. The goal stated in the argument is confidence that ineligible ballots are not counted.

**Against.** The argument says the added step will not meaningfully improve security and will leave eligible ballots uncounted when a number is missing or wrong. It also raises privacy concerns about putting part of a Social Security number on an envelope. It describes the current signature process as already catching the rare ineligible ballot.

The Blue Book does not quantify how many ballots would fail the new check, or how many ineligible ballots the current system misses.

## What can swing a vote

| If this is the priority | It points toward |
| --- | --- |
| A second, number-based check on the ballot type 92 percent of voters use | Yes |
| Avoiding rejections of eligible voters who skip a box or worry about the number on the envelope | No |
| Keeping the bipartisan signature review as the only mail-ballot check | No |
| Willingness to spend up to $2.7 million up front, possibly funded in part by higher business filing fees | Yes, if the check is worth that cost; No, if it is not |
| In-person voters | Unaffected. Their ID list does not change. |
