# Denver ballot summary, November 3, 2026

Use this page to see every choice that can appear on a Denver ballot, then open the linked guide for the arguments, the fiscal numbers, and the priorities that can swing a vote.

This page does not recommend a vote. “Swing” means a priority that, if it is the one you weight most heavily, points toward one side or the other. It is not a prediction.

**Council District 10** does not have its own council race on this ballot. Chris Hinds is the current member. Residents there are all in Congressional District 1, and they split across state House Districts 5, 6, and 8. The neighborhood crosswalk is in [guides/council-district-10.md](guides/council-district-10.md).

## How to read the tables

- **Who sees it.** “Every Denver voter” means the contest is on ballots citywide. “Depends on address” means the combined sample ballot prints every district’s contest, and only one of them is on your ballot.
- **Passage.** Citizen constitutional amendments generally need **55 percent**. The 2026 Blue Book states that **Amendment 87 passes with a majority**, because it both repeals the single-rate income-tax rule and enacts a new tax structure. Statutory propositions, Proposition NN, and the local questions are majority votes. A private forecast site has described Amendment 87 as needing 55 percent; the Blue Book does not.
- **Polling column.** A blank or “none located” means this review did not find a public survey of that contest. Insider win-odds and model forecasts are not polls and are kept out of the polling column. They are discussed only in the race guides, and only when a source published them.
- **Numbers.** Dollar figures and tax rates below are copied from the Blue Book, the Denver Ballot Information Booklet, or the Front Range Passenger Rail District’s election notice. They are estimates unless the source says they are actual receipts.

## Polling, stated once

As of October 28, 2026:

- The only statewide public horse-race poll of the governor’s race located here is **Magellan Strategies, July 30–August 12, 2025** (about 14 months before Election Day): Democrat 50 percent, Republican 38 percent, among registered voters, as listed by the [New York Times poll tracker](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/polls/colorado-governor-election-polls-2026.html). A secondary compilation reports the same survey as n = 1,136, Phil Weiser 50.1 percent, Victor Marx 37.8 percent.
- Later surveys that show a close Weiser–Marx race, or a Baisley lead over John Hickenlooper, were taken **inside Republican-leaning congressional districts**, not statewide and not in Denver. Impact Research, September 21–24, 2026, n = 403 likely voters in the 4th District: Marx 46, Weiser 45; Baisley 50, Hickenlooper 45. Global Strategy Group, October 14–18, 2026, n = 500 likely voters in the 5th District: Weiser 45, Marx 43. Denver voters are in the 1st District or part of the 6th. Do not treat those district numbers as a Denver or statewide result.
- No public poll of Hickenlooper versus Baisley statewide was located. [Pollitrack](https://pollitrack.com/co) said as of October 24, 2026 that none had been published.
- No public poll was located for any of the 14 statewide measures, for Ballot Issue 7A, for the Denver measures, for Denver Public Schools Ballot Issue 4A, or for the at-large council vacancy. [On Point Politics](https://onpointpolitics.us/forecast/measures/co/amendment-87) says its Amendment 87 and Proposition 137 pages have no polls underneath the forecast.

## Measures that interact

Vote these pairs with the other measure open.

| Pair | What the official analysis says | Guide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amendment 87 and Proposition 136 | 87 would tax taxable income over $500,000 at 7.4 to 8.4 percent. 136 would cap the income-tax rate at 4.4 percent. If both pass, the Blue Book says the usual rule is that the measure with more votes prevails, but “the exact outcome is unclear” and a court or the legislature may have to resolve it. | [87](guides/amendment-87-graduated-income-tax.md), [136](guides/proposition-136-income-tax-cap.md) |
| Proposition NN and Proposition 137 | NN lets the state keep revenue above the TABOR limit, up to a higher limit tied to school spending. 137 takes sporting-goods sales-tax revenue out of that limit. If both pass, 137 reduces what NN can actually retain. | [NN](guides/proposition-nn-k12-tabor.md), [137](guides/proposition-137-conservation.md) |

## Data gaps that apply to the whole ballot

- No address was provided, so this guide cannot mark which legislative, congressional, education, regent, RTD, or judicial questions are on one household’s ballot.
- Public horse-race polling for Denver, and for the ballot measures, was not found.
- The Denver clerk does not fact-check pro and con comments in the local booklet.
- Judicial performance narratives in the Blue Book did not come through in the text used here. Use [judicialperformance.colorado.gov](https://judicialperformance.colorado.gov) and the printed Blue Book before voting yes or no on a judge.
- Candidate questionnaires for legislative, RTD, regent, education-board, treasurer, and secretary-of-state candidates were not compiled. Those guides say so instead of inventing positions.
